CASE FILE 01 · DISSERTATION

Who Pays When the Push Goes Wrong?

THE QUESTION

When a customer is deceived into authorising a payment to a fraudster, who should carry the loss: the customer, the bank that sent the money, or the bank that received it?

THE EVIDENCE
  • Open banking (PSD2, implemented by the Payment Services Regulations 2017, and the CMA Retail Banking Order 2017) let third parties access account data and initiate payments, putting more hands on every transaction.
  • In authorised push payment (APP) fraud the victim approves the payment, so the protections built for unauthorised transactions do not reach them.
  • From 7 October 2024 the Payment Systems Regulator requires in scope Faster Payments APP fraud victims to be reimbursed in most cases: cost split 50:50 between sending and receiving firms, capped at £85,000 per claim.
THE METHOD
  • Dissertation: Open Banking and Consumer Protection in the United Kingdom (LLB, Brunel University London).
  • Doctrinal analysis of UK payment regulation, APP fraud liability and risk allocation, synthesising 60+ primary and secondary sources.
  • Statutes, regulator policy statements and case law read in the original, referenced in OSCOLA.
THE VERDICT
“Open banking widened the doors; the liability rules had to catch up. Loss should sit with those best placed to detect and stop the fraud, which is why the move from voluntary codes to mandatory, shared reimbursement matters.”

The Client Who Didn't Add Up

THE QUESTION

A new business client's account activity no longer matches its stated profile. Is this money laundering, and what must a regulated firm do about it?

THE EVIDENCE
  • Onboarding file: small import business, declared turnover £180,000 a year. Actual throughput: £1.3 million in four months.
  • Eleven round sum inbound transfers from unconnected companies, each just under £10,000.
  • Funds moved out within 48 hours to accounts in three jurisdictions. Invoices requested; none supplied.
  • Beneficial owner changed twice in a year. The new sole director is 22 with no trading history.
THE METHOD
  • Customer due diligence and enhanced due diligence under the Money Laundering Regulations 2017.
  • Red flag review against JMLSG guidance; typologies identified: structuring and pass through (“mule”) activity.
  • Output: internal escalation memo to the MLRO and a draft Suspicious Activity Report in NCA format.
THE VERDICT
“The pattern met the ‘knows or suspects’ threshold. Recommendation: escalate to the MLRO, file a SAR with the National Crime Agency under the Proceeds of Crime Act 2002, and do not tip off the client.”

CASE FILE 03 · SIMULATED COMPLIANCE REVIEW ILLUSTRATIVE · ANONYMISED FIRM

Fine Print, Big Gaps

THE QUESTION

A fintech's customer terms look compliant on paper. Do they actually deliver the outcomes the FCA's Consumer Duty demands?

THE EVIDENCE
  • Fees for a foreign payment split across three documents; the total cost is never shown in one place.
  • The fraud warning sits on page 14 of the terms; nothing appears at the moment a customer makes a payment.
  • Account freeze process: no stated timeline and no named contact route for the customer.
  • No sign that the customer journey was tested with people in vulnerable circumstances.
THE METHOD
  • Documents mapped against the Consumer Duty (FCA Principle 12 and PRIN 2A, in force for open products since 31 July 2023).
  • Scored against the four outcomes: products & services, price & value, consumer understanding, consumer support.
  • Illustrative exercise on an anonymised UK fintech's public documents; findings written as a gap analysis memo.
THE VERDICT
“Compliant on paper, weak on outcomes. Fix list: one page fee summary, fraud warning at the point of payment, published freeze timelines, and vulnerability testing before launch.”
